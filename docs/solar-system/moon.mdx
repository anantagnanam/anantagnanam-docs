<Manjari>
Two lovely questions! Let me give you one clue each. First: does a planet have to orbit another planet? Second: what happens to your shadow when someone stands in front of your lamp?
</Manjari>

<Astra>
The Moon is not a planet — it is a **natural satellite**, a world that orbits a planet, just as the planets orbit the Sun. The Moon orbits Earth.

And about its shape: the Moon is always round. What changes is how much of its sunlit side we can see. As the Moon travels around Earth, the Sun lights different parts of it, and we call those changing views the **phases of the Moon**.
</Astra>

## The Phases of the Moon

Over about 29.5 days, the Moon completes one full cycle of phases:

- **New Moon** — the Moon is between Earth and the Sun; we see the dark side, so it is nearly invisible
- **Crescent** — a thin sliver appears
- **First Quarter** — half of the Moon is lit
- **Gibbous** — more than half, but not full
- **Full Moon** — the whole sunlit face turns toward us
- …then the cycle repeats, growing thinner again

<Callout type="tip" title="Try it yourself">
Stand in a dark room with a ball and a torch (a phone flashlight works!). Shine the torch on the ball and slowly walk around it. The "phases" you see are exactly why the Moon changes shape — you are seeing different amounts of its lit side.
</Callout>

## A Gentle Pull on the Oceans

The Moon is close enough that its gravity tugs on Earth — most visibly on the oceans. That pull is what creates **tides**: the gentle rise and fall of the sea, roughly twice a day. When the Sun and Moon pull together, the tides are especially high.

<DidYouKnow>
The Moon always shows us the same face. It spins on its axis at exactly the same rate it orbits Earth — a gentle lockstep called tidal locking. The far side of the Moon was a complete mystery until spacecraft photographed it!
</DidYouKnow>
