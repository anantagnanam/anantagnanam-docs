<Astra>
Saturn's rings are not solid at all. They are made of countless pieces of **ice and rock** — from grains of dust to chunks as big as houses. All of them orbit Saturn like tiny moons. As for why Saturn's rings are so grand, scientists think they may come from comets or moons torn apart by Saturn's gravity long ago.
</Astra>

## Rings That Would Vanish in a Handshake

Saturn's rings are enormous — they span about 280,000 km across — yet they are astonishingly thin, in places only about 10 meters thick. If you scaled Saturn down to a dinner plate, its rings would be thinner than a sheet of paper.

<Callout type="tip" title="The rings are busy">
The rings are not still. Their icy pieces jostle, collide, and drift — constantly changing. Saturn's gravity sculpts them into bands, gaps, and ripples.
</Callout>

## A Moon with Weather

Saturn's largest moon, **Titan**, is a world of its own — with a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane. It is so interesting that a spacecraft has parachuted down through its orange haze to explore it.

<DidYouKnow>
You can see Saturn's rings with a simple pair of binoculars from Earth! Galileo saw them in 1610 — though his telescope was so small that he thought Saturn looked like a planet with two handles, or even two moons.
</DidYouKnow>
