Mars looks red because its soil is full of **iron oxide** — the same rusty compound that forms on old iron left out in the rain. Martian dust has been rusting for billions of years, and winds blow that reddish dust across the whole planet.

## A Land of Giants

Mars is a small, cold desert world, but it holds the most dramatic scenery in the Solar System:

- **Olympus Mons** — the tallest volcano known, roughly three times the height of Mount Everest
- **Valles Marineris** — a canyon system so long it would stretch across the entire United States
- **Polar ice caps** — white caps of water ice and frozen carbon dioxide that grow and shrink with the seasons

<Callout type="warning" title="A cold desert">
Mars is far colder than Earth — its average temperature is around -60 °C. And its air is thin, mostly carbon dioxide. You could not breathe there without a spacesuit.
</Callout>

## The Search for Water

Mars is dry now, but it was not always. Spacecraft have found dried-up riverbeds, lakebeds, and minerals that only form in water. Long ago, Mars may have had rivers, lakes, and maybe even oceans.

That is why scientists are so curious — where there was water, there may once have been life. Today, rovers roll across its surface, drilling into the dust and sending home messages from another world.

<DidYouKnow>
Mars has seasons just like Earth! Because its axis is tilted, Mars experiences spring, summer, autumn, and winter — though each season lasts about twice as long as ours.
</DidYouKnow>

<WhyItMatters>
Studying Mars helps scientists understand how planets change over time — and whether environments beyond Earth might once have supported life. Every rover wheel-track is a step toward answering one of the oldest questions: are we alone?
</WhyItMatters>
